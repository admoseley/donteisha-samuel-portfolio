My Future, My Fit: Discovering Your Ideal Career Journey

Instructional materials and an aligned assessment guiding high school students through self-assessment, career research, and comparison — so they build a personalized post-graduation plan grounded in their own strengths and values.

DS
D. Samuel • 2026 • Concept project

High school students often face a version of the same problem: they're asked to decide about their futures before they've had the chance to systematically explore what those futures could look like. This project addresses that gap through a structured, learner-centered set of instructional materials that guide students through three interconnected steps — assessing their strengths, researching careers, and comparing options — before committing to a plan.

Course objectives

By the end of this learning experience, students will be able to:

  • Assess strengths — complete a self-assessment tool and interpret the results to identify personal strengths, interests, and values that connect to career preferences
  • Research careers — investigate at least two career options by examining job duties, required education or training, and salary expectations
  • Compare options — evaluate career choices using a structured comparison tool and justify a preferred pathway using evidence from their research and self-assessment

Many paths to success

The materials open by explicitly naming what students often aren't told: there is no single "right" path after high school. The instructional design intentionally presents four equally valid trajectories — college degrees, apprenticeships, military service, and direct workforce entry — before any career exploration activity begins. This framing matters: students who see themselves reflected in at least one pathway are more likely to engage seriously with the research and comparison steps that follow.

Before students can explore where they're going, they need to believe their direction is a real option — not just a fallback.

Step 1: Self-assessment

Students complete a structured self-assessment to surface four categories of information about themselves: strengths, interests, values, and a short personal summary. The assessment is designed to be accessible and non-judgmental — the goal is honest reflection, not a test with right answers.

After completing the assessment, students respond to guided reflection prompts around career alignment: How do my results connect to the careers I've heard of? Are there careers that surprised me? What do I want my daily work life to feel like? These prompts help students connect quantitative self-assessment scores to qualitative personal meaning — a step that assessment tools alone can't accomplish.

Step 2: Career research

With self-assessment data in hand, students investigate careers using a structured three-part research framework:

  • Job duties — what does this work actually look like day to day?
  • Required education and training — what credentials or experience are needed to enter and advance?
  • Salary expectations — what is the realistic earning range, and how does it vary by region, experience, and specialization?

Students also participate in an expert panel component, reflecting on key takeaways about networking and hands-on experience, surprising paths in emerging industries, and strategies for connecting with professionals (including LinkedIn outreach). The reflection prompt "Connecting the Dots of Discovery" asks students to identify emerging themes across what their strengths, interests, and research have in common.

Step 3: Comparing careers

The career comparison worksheet is the design centerpiece of this project. Students evaluate up to three career options across a structured rubric:

  • Interest level (1–5 scale)
  • Education and training required
  • Daily tasks and work environment
  • Skills needed
  • Salary range
  • Pros and cons for their specific situation
  • Support resources available

After completing the worksheet, students answer three synthesis questions: Which career fits best and why? How did their strengths influence the decision? What is one concrete next step they can take now?

The comparison is followed by a Career Exploration Reflection Worksheet — five structured questions completed after any career interviews or informational conversations — designed to surface what students learned that they couldn't have found in a search engine.

The worksheet isn't a test — it's a thinking tool. The goal is to teach students how to make a reasoned decision, not just to pick a career.

Action planning

The final phase moves students from decision to commitment. The action planning grid organizes next steps across four time horizons:

  • Immediate steps — things a student can do this week
  • Short-term goals — actions within the next few months
  • Long-term objectives — where they want to be in 1–3 years
  • Resources needed — people, programs, or tools that can help

The closing message of the learning experience is intentional: Your Pathway is Clear. Not because the future is certain — but because the student has now explored, researched, compared, and planned. That process itself is the outcome.

Design rationale

This project was built using backward design — the assessments and reflection worksheets were designed first, then the instructional materials were built to prepare students to complete them successfully. Every activity connects directly to one of the three core objectives. Accessibility was a non-negotiable constraint: materials use plain language, avoid jargon, and present information in formats that work for students across a range of reading levels and prior knowledge.
