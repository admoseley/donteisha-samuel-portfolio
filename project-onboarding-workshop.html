Onboarding Workshop for New Instructional Designers

A five-day workshop grounding new ID team members in design history, foundational theory, emerging trends, and the full ADDIE framework — built for practice, not just lecture.

DS
D. Samuel • April 2026 • Concept project

New instructional designers often enter teams with inconsistent foundations — strong in some areas, uncertain in others. This workshop addresses that gap with a structured, five-day onboarding experience that moves participants from the roots of the field through practical application of the ADDIE model.

Workshop objectives

By the end of the week, participants are expected to:

  • Define instructional design and describe its scope across education and workplace contexts (Reiser, 2001)
  • Explain key historical roots of instructional design, including its military training origins, and identify major theorists that shaped the field (Reiser, 2001)
  • Analyze current trends — technology integration, learner-centered and empathetic approaches, and learning analytics — and predict implications for future learning (Ferguson, 2012)
  • Apply the ADDIE framework by mapping a learning problem to phase deliverables, from analysis through evaluation (Branch, 2009)
  • Justify design decisions using empirical research (e.g., retrieval practice, spaced practice) and recommend ways to build a continuous-learning culture on the ID team (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008; Cepeda et al., 2006)

Session 1: Introduction to instructional design

Instructional design is a systematic process aimed at creating effective educational experiences, evolving from early methodologies to contemporary applications in diverse learning environments. Its scope encompasses corporate training, higher education, and e-learning, with a consistent focus on learner needs and innovative instructional strategies.

The field's roots trace directly to World War II, when the military urgently needed systematic training methods to prepare large numbers of personnel quickly and consistently. That wartime pressure produced the foundational thinking that still shapes modern practice — a systematic approach that incorporates psychological theory, adapts to diverse environments, and centers learner needs rather than content delivery.

The field was born from necessity — the military needed people trained fast, correctly, and at scale. That urgency became the blueprint.

Session 2: Foundational theorists

Three theoretical lineages anchor the field:

Skinner & Mager — behaviorist foundations. Skinner's principles emphasize observable behaviors as the focus of learning, promoting structured environments where desired outcomes can be systematically achieved. Mager extended this with his approach to objective-setting: clear, measurable outcomes that ensure learners understand the expectations and goals of their instructional experiences. Together, these theories encourage effective learning strategies that promote engagement and mastery through targeted behavioral objectives.

Bloom & Gagné — learning domains and conditions. Bloom's Taxonomy categorizes cognitive skills into six levels — from Remember through Create — promoting a structured approach to assessing learning outcomes. Gagné's Conditions of Learning emphasize the necessity of specific instructional events to facilitate effective learning, ensuring that learners are prepared and engaged throughout the educational process. Both frameworks help designers create focused and measurable learning objectives in diverse contexts.

Merrill's First Principles. Merrill's principles emphasize real-world relevance, ensuring that learning experiences are meaningful and applicable to participants' everyday tasks and challenges. Incorporating active learning strategies fosters deeper understanding and retention of knowledge through hands-on activities and collaboration among peers.

Theory without application is trivia. The goal is to use these frameworks to make better decisions — not to cite them.

Session 3: Current trends in instructional design

Technology integration. Integrating technology in instructional design enhances engagement and accessibility, enabling designers to create more interactive and personalized learning experiences for diverse audiences. Learner-centered design prioritizes individual needs and preferences, fostering environments where learners take ownership of their educational journey. Instructional design must equip learners with essential skills for the digital age — emphasizing critical thinking, collaboration, and adaptability (Means et al., 2014).

Accessibility and microlearning. Universal Design for Learning (UDL) emphasizes flexible educational environments that accommodate diverse learners through multiple means of engagement, representation, and action. Microlearning breaks content into bite-sized, digestible modules that promote retention and allow learners to access information just-in-time — aligning with modern attention spans and busy schedules (Hug, 2005). Technology tools such as mobile apps and online platforms facilitate flexible learning paths.

Learning analytics. Learning analytics provide valuable insights into learner performance, enabling instructional designers to make informed decisions about content and instructional strategies. Using predictive analytics allows designers to anticipate learner needs and outcomes, facilitating tailored educational experiences that enhance engagement and effectiveness (Siemens & Long, 2011; Ferguson, 2012).

Session 4: The ADDIE model

ADDIE is the core process framework for this team's design work. Each phase has distinct outputs that drive the next phase forward — understanding the purpose of each stage prevents shortcuts that cost time later.

Analysis — Identify learning needs by understanding learner characteristics, analyzing the environment where learning will occur, and assessing gaps between current and desired performance. Deliverable: needs analysis document.

Design — Define clear learning objectives to guide the design process, determine content development approach, and plan assessment strategies to measure learner progress. Deliverable: design document / blueprint.

Development — Create the actual materials, focusing on engaging and effective learning content. Work with stakeholders to ensure alignment and relevance, and implement review processes to enhance material effectiveness. Deliverable: draft modules or course materials.

Implementation — Deliver the training with essential strategies for effective facilitation. Create a supportive atmosphere that enhances learner engagement and retention. Establish feedback mechanisms to capture data throughout the training process. Deliverable: live or deployed training.

Evaluation — Assess instructional effectiveness using multiple methods that provide insights into learner performance outcomes. Use constructive feedback to inform future design improvements and revisions. Deliverable: evaluation report with recommended revisions.

Each ADDIE phase is only as strong as the phase before it. Rushing analysis means the design is guesswork — and guesswork costs everyone time.

Research-based practices

Retrieval practice. Retrieval practice helps strengthen memory by encouraging learners to recall information, enhancing long-term retention and understanding through active engagement with the material. Spaced learning involves interleaving study sessions over time, allowing for better retention and comprehension by giving learners time to process and consolidate knowledge. Incorporating both techniques into instructional design fosters a more effective learning environment, promoting engagement and deeper understanding (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008; Cepeda et al., 2006).

Building a continuous-learning culture. Continuous learning fosters a culture of improvement, encouraging employees to regularly update their skills and knowledge, leading to enhanced organizational performance and adaptability. Implementing mentorship programs and regular training sessions can promote continuous learning, ensuring that team members remain engaged and empowered to pursue professional development opportunities.
